Heating and cooling for Buckeye Lake homes

Buckeye Lake started as a summer resort, and the village still shows it. Many homes near the water began as seasonal cottages and were winterized and expanded over the years. Others are newer year-round houses built to take advantage of the lake. Both share a few things: crawl spaces, humidity and heating systems squeezed into tight spaces.

What we see around the lake

  • Converted cottages with small furnaces, electric heat or wall units that were never designed for full-time winter living.
  • Crawl space ductwork exposed to damp air, with rusted connections and missing insulation.
  • High summer humidity that an oversized AC cannot remove, leaving homes cool but clammy.
  • Outdoor units exposed to wind off the water and debris in storms.
  • Manufactured homes with specialized furnaces that need the correct replacement models.
From the field

A lot of the lake cottages we work on were expanded two or three times, and the heating never kept up. The original furnace serves the original rooms, and the additions get whatever reaches the end of the duct. Ductless mini-splits in the added rooms are usually the cleanest way to even out those houses.

Humidity is the summer problem

Living next to a lake means humid summers. An air conditioner only removes moisture while it runs long enough for water to drip off its coil. Oversized systems cycle off too quickly, and the house ends up cool but damp, with musty smells and condensation on vents. Correct sizing, a variable-speed system or a dehumidification setting often solves what a bigger AC never could.

From the field

We regularly find crawl spaces near the lake where the AC's condensate line simply ends under the house. All summer, gallons of water go straight into the crawl space, which then feeds the humidity problem inside. Rerouting that line is a small job with a surprisingly large effect.

Seasonal homes and freeze protection

Some homes near the lake sit empty for weeks at a time in winter. An unoccupied house with a furnace that quits can freeze pipes and flood before anyone notices. If your home is seasonal or you travel in winter, set the thermostat no lower than about 55 degrees, shut off the main water supply if you will be gone for a long stretch, and consider a Wi-Fi thermostat or temperature sensor that alerts your phone if the house gets cold.

A fall tune-up before you leave is especially worthwhile for seasonal homes, since nobody is there to notice a problem early. If you come back to a cold house, see our troubleshooting guide or call us.

Questions we hear

HVAC questions from Buckeye Lake homeowners

Can you heat a converted lake cottage year-round?

Yes. Depending on the layout, the best option may be a correctly sized furnace or heat pump with new ductwork, or ductless mini-splits that avoid running ducts through a tight crawl space.

Why does my lake house feel damp even with the AC on?

Most often the AC is oversized and short cycling, or condensate is draining into the crawl space. Correct sizing and proper drainage usually fix it.

Do you work on manufactured home furnaces?

Yes. Manufactured homes require furnaces specifically approved for them, and we install and repair those models.

Is a heat pump good for a home near the lake?

Yes, especially for all-electric homes. Cold-climate models heat well through our winters and dehumidify in summer.
